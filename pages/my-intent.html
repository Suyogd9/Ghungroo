Essay No. 02

What I am, in fact, signing up for

You called me "khup samjudar." This page is what's behind that word.

Promises in this format are dangerous — they tend to be either grand and meaningless, or small and condescending. I've tried to keep these specific and verifiable. Five things, in descending order of difficulty, because anyone can promise the easy things.

i.

Your parents become my parents.

Not in the abstract Indian-wedding sense. In the operational sense. The Mother's Day call, the retirement surprise, the worry on the hard nights — those are now my problems too. The line you said — "it's the last thing on earth I'd want to see, them being hurt" — applies to me now also. Pune trips will be frequent, video calls will be daily, and on the days that ask for more, we'll figure out more. The complicated family piece — we'll work on it patiently, together, on your timeline.

ii.

Your work is not on pause after marriage.

The Arangetram. The channel. The business you've been thinking about. None of these get downgraded to hobbies because we got married. The H-4 window is for runway-building. EAD is for takeoff. I'd like to be your first subscriber, first sponsor lead, and — ego aside — first investor when the time comes. The frame I want is: you have a career, I have a career, and we are good for each other's careers.

Partnership, not permission. I get a vote, never a veto.
iii.

You don't need to ask me for permission for anything.

Your friend circles, your time, your money, what you study, what you wear, what you collect — these are yours. I get a vote on the things that affect us both; I do not get a veto on the things that are yours. The ten-to-seven you've drawn around your work life stays. Your alcohol collection — which I still find delightful and slightly mysterious — stays. The space you've built for yourself is the space I want to step into, not the space I want to redecorate.

iv.

Direct, always. No games, no guessing.

You said you don't like overly sweet boys. I promise the over-performing politeness of the first month is — well — the first month. The actual me is more "baat toh sahi hei" than "aap bahut acche ho." If you're upset, please say so. If you're delighted, please say so. If you're unsure, please say so. I won't overinterpret your moods, but I will always notice them. (As you said: "all days are not same." Noted, accepted, baked in.)

v.

The boring adult things are mine to handle.

The H-4 paperwork. The taxes. The insurance. The new-apartment setup. The first six months of adjusting to a country you didn't grow up in. None of this should be your cognitive load. Your job is to write poetry when poetry shows up, to make food when food shows up, to dance when the Arangetram approaches, and to call your parents often. I'll take everything else.

This list is a starting point, not a contract. We'll add to it together, edit it together, occasionally violate it together. But it's an honest beginning — and an honest beginning is the part most people skip.
